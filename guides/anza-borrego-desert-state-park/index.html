Where dogs can go

Anza-Borrego follows the California State Parks rule: dogs on leash in campgrounds, picnic areas and on roads, never on trails. The twist here is that about 500 miles of the park's roads are dirt jeep roads through washes and badlands, and dogs are welcome on all of them. Add free dispersed car camping almost anywhere and this is one of the better state parks for a dog, as long as you come between November and March and respect the cactus, snakes and heat.

  • Dirt roads open to vehicles

    This is the big one. Roughly 500 miles of unpaved jeep roads cross the park, through washes, badlands and palm-lined canyons, and a leashed dog can walk any of them with you. Pick a quiet one and you have the desert to yourselves.

  • Paved roads and road shoulders

    Highway S-22, S-3, Highway 78 and the paved park roads. Fine for a walk, but watch for fast traffic and hot asphalt.

  • Visitor Center to Campground Trail

    The one official dog trail in the park: a 1.2-mile round trip on a wide, mostly level path between the Visitor Center and Borrego Palm Canyon Campground. The short native plant garden paths around the Visitor Center are also fine.

  • All developed campgrounds

    Borrego Palm Canyon, Tamarisk Grove, Bow Willow, Fish Creek, Vern Whitaker Horse Camp, Arroyo Salado, Yaqui Well and Culp Valley. Dogs must sleep in your tent or vehicle.

  • Dispersed camping

    Anza-Borrego lets you camp for free almost anywhere you can legally drive, as long as you stay next to the road. The same rule applies to dogs: they can go where the car can go.

  • Picnic areas and parking lots

    Including the Visitor Center, Tamarisk Grove and the paved overlooks along S-22.

  • Borrego Springs town

    Christmas Circle park, the Galleta Meadows metal sculptures (on private land, dog-friendly) and most outdoor patios welcome leashed dogs.

Where dogs are not allowed

  • Every hiking trail

    Borrego Palm Canyon Trail, Hellhole Canyon, Slot Canyon, Wind Caves, Pictograph Trail, Mountain Palm Springs and every other foot trail. Carrying the dog does not make it legal.

  • Backcountry and wilderness

    Anything off the road. More than half the park is designated wilderness, and dogs may not leave the roadway there.

  • Wildflower fields

    In a bloom year, rangers enforce this. Dogs stay on the road while you look at the flowers.

  • Visitor Center building

    Shaded benches and the garden paths outside are fine. Service dogs only inside.

  • Outside your tent or car at night

    The park is strict about this. Coyotes patrol campgrounds after dark and have taken small dogs.

  • Unattended at a campsite

    Rangers will cite you, and in summer a dog left in a car can die within minutes.

Dog-friendly walks

Every walk here is one your dog is allowed on. Distances are round trip unless it says loop.

Good places to hang out

  • Fonts Point

    Sunrise over the badlands from the end of the sand road. Park, leash up and walk to the rim edge together.

  • Borrego Palm Canyon Campground

    The main campground, with shade ramadas, flush toilets and showers. The campground loops make an easy evening dog walk.

  • Galleta Meadows sculptures, Borrego Springs

    130 metal sculptures of dinosaurs, camels and a 350-foot serpent scattered over open desert just outside the park. Leashed dogs welcome.

  • Culp Valley

    A free primitive campground at 3,400 feet off Montezuma Grade. Around 10 degrees cooler than the valley floor, which matters in the shoulder seasons.

  • Tamarisk Grove

    Small shaded campground under tamarisk trees at the Highway 78 and S-3 junction. Quieter than Palm Canyon and a good base for the Split Mountain road.

The rules, in plain words

  1. Keep your dog on a leash no longer than 6 feet, at all times, everywhere in the park.
  2. Dogs may use campgrounds, picnic areas, paved roads and dirt roads open to vehicles. They may not use any trail, except the Visitor Center to Campground Trail.
  3. Dogs stay out of the backcountry, the wilderness and the wildflower fields, even if you carry them.
  4. At night your dog must be inside your tent or vehicle, never tied up outside.
  5. Never leave a dog unattended, in a car or at a campsite. Desert cars reach deadly temperatures in minutes.
  6. Pick up waste and pack it out. There are no trash cans on the dirt roads.
  7. Rangers can ask for proof of vaccination and licence, and can turn away noisy or aggressive dogs.
  8. Call the park at (760) 767-5311 before a summer trip. The Visitor Center is only open weekends from June through September.
  9. Service dogs are allowed everywhere; emotional support animals follow the pet rules.

Camping and places to stay

Camping with your dog

  • Borrego Palm Canyon CampgroundThe main campground, 2 miles from Borrego Springs. About 120 sites with showers and hookups. Reserve on ReserveCalifornia; it fills on winter weekends and during wildflower season.
  • Tamarisk Grove Campground27 shaded sites near Highway 78. Reserve on ReserveCalifornia. Pets must be in a tent or vehicle at night.
  • Primitive campgroundsCulp Valley, Arroyo Salado, Fish Creek, Yaqui Well, Bow Willow and others. Free or low cost, first come first served, no water. Dogs welcome on the same rules.
  • Dispersed roadside campingFree. Park next to any open dirt road away from a water source and camp. The best way to give a dog room without breaking a rule.

Pet-friendly lodging

  • La Casa del Zorro Resort and Spa, Borrego SpringsThe classic desert resort. Third-party listings show up to 2 dogs under 80 lb with a nightly fee. Confirm with the hotel before booking.
  • Borrego Springs Resort and SpaListed as taking 2 pets of any size with a nightly pet fee.
  • Stanlunds Inn and Suites, Borrego SpringsSimple motel with designated pet rooms and a small nightly fee.
  • Palm Canyon Hotel and RV Resort, Borrego SpringsWestern-themed hotel and RV park next to the park entrance. Pet-friendly, with a dirt relief area.

When to go

  • Summer heat (May to September)

    Daytime highs run 105 to 120 degrees and the ground burns paws by mid-morning. Dogs have died here from heat. If you must come, walk before 8 a.m. and stay in air conditioning the rest of the day.

  • Winter and spring (November to April)

    The season to visit. Days in the 60s and 70s, cold nights. Wildflower bloom, usually late February to March, brings big crowds and full campgrounds in a good year.

  • Cholla cactus

    Jumping cholla segments stick to paws, noses and tails along every road edge. Carry pliers or a comb to pull them out, and keep the leash short through cactus patches.

  • Flash floods

    Washes like Fish Creek and Coyote Canyon can flood after rain even when the sky above you is clear. Do not camp in a wash bottom.

Wildlife and hazards

  • Rattlesnakes are active from March to October, often on warm road surfaces at dusk. Pet dogs have died from bites in this park.
  • Coyotes come into campgrounds every night and will take a small dog. Keep dogs inside the tent or car after dark.
  • Peninsular bighorn sheep are endangered. Coyote Canyon closes in summer so they can reach water; keep your dog well back if you see them.
  • Scorpions and tarantulas hide under rocks and shoes. Shake out bedding and bowls each morning.
